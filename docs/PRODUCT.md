# Product

## Register

product

## Users

People with an idea for a physical part and no CAD skills: comfortable with
Claude (desktop app or terminal), curious about making things, usually new to
3D design and printing. They don't own a printer; parts are ordered from print
services in resin, plastic or steel. They work beside a Claude chat, on a
laptop, often with a trackpad.

The job on the review page: look at the part Claude just made, understand
whether it's right, point at what's wrong, and hand that back to Claude for
the next iteration. Then do it again, version after version, until it's ready
to order.

## Product Purpose

chat-to-3d-workbench turns a conversation into an orderable part: "the chat
is the CAD program". The review page is where the user and Claude meet over
the model. Success is a short loop: a pin and a note on the model become the
next version without screenshots, arrows or a second tool, and the user
trusts what they order because the checks are visible when they matter.

## Brand Personality

Calm workshop bench. Precise, quiet, quietly expert; technical but friendly.
The part is the subject and everything else is the inspection sheet beside
it. The user should feel in control and unhurried: nothing shouts unless it
needs fixing before ordering.

## Anti-references

- CAD software: dense toolbars, tiny icons, modal settings, jargon on every
  control. This is exactly what users are escaping.
- Generic SaaS dashboard: stat tiles, card grids, gradients, a page that
  could be any product.

## Design Principles

1. **The model first, feedback second, numbers on demand.** The 3D view and
   the user's pins carry the page; findings and process notes are there when
   asked for, not in the way.
2. **Every control shows its effect.** If switching a view changes nothing
   visible, the page says why ("nothing under 0.8 mm") instead of leaving the
   user guessing.
3. **Close the loop.** Feedback should reach Claude in one action, with a
   visible confirmation that it did.
4. **Stable under the hand.** Controls don't move when content changes;
   switching versions keeps the camera, the layout and the user's place.
5. **Plain words.** Explain what a colour or a number means for ordering,
   in the user's terms, the first time it appears.

## Accessibility & Inclusion

WCAG 2.2 AA contrast in light and dark themes, full keyboard use, respects
reduced motion. Trackpad friendly: turning, moving and zooming work well on a
MacBook trackpad, not only a three-button mouse, and the controls say how.
